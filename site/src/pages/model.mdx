# The core tree model

This page walks through the **tree rules only** — ownership, the two exposure
channels, availability, and the rule that a file may import exactly what is
available in its module. Importer contexts and exposure tags (testing,
browser) are a separate subject and appear nowhere here.

The example is a small online shop: `shop` at the root, with `catalog`
(`search`, `inventory`), `checkout` (`cart`, `payment`) and `shipping`
underneath. Eight modules, eight symbols, and five decisions that between them
are the entire access policy of the application.

Two things to learn before reading anything else:

- **An arrowhead landing on a module means that module may import that
  symbol.** Reach is shown, never labelled.
- **A filled dot means somebody decided.** One dot per decision, however many
  arrivals it produces — so counting the dots along a symbol's path counts the
  consents that made it legal.

Three symbols are traced in their own color, because their reach was decided
by a module other than their owner: `ProductId`, `PaymentApi` and `CartApi`.
**Click any of them** — in the legend, or on the row inside a node that lists
them — to isolate that symbol's chain, dim everything else, and reveal every

## R1 — Parent composes a direct child

`inventory` exposes `reserveStock` to its parent, so `catalog` may import it;
`cart` exposes `CartApi` to its parent, so `checkout` may import it. That is
rule 2, and it buys the parent exactly what the child offered.

It is not reciprocal and it does not spill sideways. `search` may not import
`reserveStock`, `payment` may not import `CartApi`, and `checkout` may not
import `retryQueue` at all — `payment` never exposed it, so it never entered
the exposure system. There is no implicit public surface that a parent can
always reach into.

## R2 — Domain helpers and concepts

`catalog` exposes `SkuRules` to its descendants, so `search` and `inventory`
may import it. That is rule 3, with the grant made by the symbol's own owner.

A grant is bounded by the granter's own subtree, in every direction:
`checkout` may not import `SkuRules`, and neither may `shop`. Exposing
downward and exposing upward are independent channels, and doing one is never
doing the other.

## R3 — Application-wide contracts

`shop` exposes `Money` and `formatDate` to its descendants, and since `shop`'s
subtree is the whole application, every other module may import them. This is
the same rule as R2, applied at a different altitude.

Nothing is out of reach here, and that is the lesson: the root has no special
power. It is an ordinary module whose subtree happens to be everything. Note
also that both symbols ride one lane — a lane is a decision, not a symbol.

## R4 — Cross-branch (sibling, cousin) import

`payment` exposes `PaymentApi` up to `checkout`; `checkout` re-exposes it to
its own subtree; `cart`, a sibling of `payment`, may now import it. A sibling
import is not a channel — it is two ordinary hops, one up and one down, each
decided by the module that made it.

So the familiar "lowest common ancestor" rule is a consequence, not a rule: the
ancestor is simply where the up-chain turns into a down-grant. `shipping` sits
outside `checkout`'s subtree, so the same symbol is refused there.

## R5 — Closed sibling families

`shipping` owns nothing and exports nothing. It receives `shop`'s grants, gives
nobody anything, and its existence changes no other module's ceiling — growing
the tree never widens anybody's access.

Siblings are strangers by default. `search` and `inventory` sit in one family
and still cannot import each other's symbols, because `inventory` exposed
`reserveStock` upward only and `catalog` did not pass it back down. `payment`
keeps `retryQueue` closed even to its own parent.

## R6 — App-wide child-owned library

`catalog` exposes `ProductId` to `shop`, and `shop` re-exposes it to its whole
subtree. Two decisions, seven arrivals, application-wide reach — earned hop by
hop rather than declared anywhere.

`shop`'s node lists `ProductId` under **holds**, not **owns**. Routing a
symbol never transfers ownership, and `shop` could have declined: `checkout` is
the exact counter-image, holding `CartApi` and passing it nowhere. Holding is
not granting.

Compare `ProductId` with `reserveStock`. Their owners made the *same* decision
— expose to parent, and nothing else — and their reaches ended up maximally
different. Neither owner decided that outcome; the parent did. Exposing a
symbol upward is a single channel that cedes its onward routing.

:::note[Why `search` may import `ProductId`]

`catalog` owns `ProductId` and exposed it **only to its parent** — so `catalog`
never gave its own children anything. `search` may import it anyway, because
`shop` sent it back down into *every* branch, including the branch it came up
through.

This is deliberate. A descendant grant is uniform: it reaches the whole
subtree, with no exclusion for the providing branch and no provenance carried
on routed symbols. A family that does not want a symbol reachable from inside
itself resolves that by not exposing it upward at all — which is exactly what
`inventory` and `cart` did with `reserveStock` and `CartApi`.

It is the one permission whose answer surprises people, which is why the
diagram draws it as an explicit allowed arc rather than leaving it to be read
off the flow.

:::

## Two properties worth naming

**Subdivision invariance.** Descendant grants address subtrees, not shapes.
The dashed what-if note inside `shipping` says it: splitting that module into
`rates` and `labels` would change nothing either of them may import.

**Locality.** Every decision in the picture was made by a module the symbol
was available in, about exactly one hop — its parent — or about its own subtree. No
arrow names a module outside the decider's immediate family, and there is no
sibling channel, no root privilege and no depth-limited audience.

## Where the precision lives

This page is a walkthrough. The normative document is the cross-module
importability rules specification, which ships with the repository at
`docs/model/cross-module-importability-rules.md`. It states the rule exactly,
defines availability, adds the contextual rules for test and browser
contexts that this page leaves out, and records the features deliberately
rejected. Where this page and the specification appear to disagree, the
specification is right.
