# The core model

The ramify core model is a small set of rules for managing a hierarchical,
multi-file module system in TypeScript, as explained in
[why modularity](/modularity). The concepts are formally defined in the
[glossary](/glossary).

**NB** This page explains the model in its simplest form through examples,
ignoring tags. Tags can restrict the availability of
exposed symbols, as explained in [the tags page](/tags).

## Example 1: Decomposing a simple app

The example app has two different domains: `invoicing` and `shipping`. They
share some concepts, but otherwise they're mostly independent and deserve
their own modules. Rather than showing the finished app at once, we'll build
it up in three steps, starting from its simplest corner. (Symbols are
self-explanatory, with `PascalCase` for types and `camelCase` for values.)

### Step 1: `shipping` - sharing between parent and child

`shipping` delegates its hardest problem to a `routingOptimization`
sub-module, which implements `optimizeRoute` against the `ShipmentPlan` type
that `shipping` itself defines. Here is that fragment alone, under the app's
(so far empty) root:

<ModelDiagram definition={example1aDiagram} />

Every box is a [**module**](/glossary#ramify-module): a directory declared as
a module root. The module hierarchy is a **tree** - `app` contains
`shipping`, which contains `routingOptimization` - and every source file
[**belongs**](/glossary#files-belonging-to-a-module) to exactly one module.
Inside a module, ramify changes nothing: files export and import exactly as
TypeScript always did. When a file exports a symbol, the module it belongs to
[**owns**](/glossary#module-owned-symbol) that symbol - `shipping` owns
`ShipmentPlan`, and `routingOptimization` owns `optimizeRoute`: the first
thing each box lists.

Crossing a module boundary is where the model begins, and its whole content
is one rule: a file may import a symbol exactly when the symbol is
[**available**](/glossary#module-available-symbol) in the file's module. A
module's own symbols are always available in it; everything else is **closed
by default**, and becomes available only if some module
[**exposes**](/glossary#module-exposed-symbol) it. Here, `shipping` is
exposing `ShipmentPlan` to its descendants, and that's why `ShipmentPlan` is
available in `routingOptimization`.

Exposure comes in exactly two forms - a module may expose any symbol it owns
or receives **to its parent** (available one level up, marked `▲`) or **to
its descendants** (available in its whole subtree, marked `▼`). Those are
the only decisions the model knows, and each one is drawn as a **filled
dot**. This fragment has one of each: the `ShipmentPlan` exposure just
described, and `routingOptimization` exposing `optimizeRoute` to its parent.
The second thing each box lists is what the module
[**receives**](/glossary#module-received-symbol): the symbols exposed to it
by a direct child or by a proper ancestor, each row naming the module it
came from. `optimizeRoute` arrives in `shipping` as `from
routingOptimization`, and `ShipmentPlan` arrives in `routingOptimization` as
`from shipping`.

And notice `app`: it receives nothing. Neither symbol was exposed any
further, so neither reaches the root. The default everywhere, for every
symbol, is "goes no further" - reach ends where decisions end.

The two channels are also asymmetric, and `ShipmentPlan` is the witness.
Exposing to descendants is final and bounded: one decision, whole subtree,
nothing ceded - and no way out, because only an exposure to the parent could
ever carry a symbol out of its subtree. So `app` is not allowed to import
`ShipmentPlan` while `shipping`'s descendants are: the **parent** is the
outsider of an exposure to descendants, root included.

The diagrams on this page are live. **Click any traced symbol**, in the
legend or on a row that lists it, to isolate its chain: everything else dims,
the symbol's flows move the way its exposures travel, and its arrivals blink
in every box it reached. Click the background to clear the selection. Each
figure also pans and zooms: drag to move around, scroll over it to zoom about
the pointer, and the buttons in the top-right corner step the zoom or restore
the whole picture.

### Step 2: `invoicing` - sharing with siblings

`invoicing` keeps its own shared vocabulary in an `invoicingLibrary` module,
whose `InvoiceModel` is used by both `invoiceComputation` and `invoicePDF`.
But look at what that requires: `invoicingLibrary` cannot hand a symbol to
its siblings, because no decision can. A decision never names a sibling,
never the root, never "everyone": a module only addresses its parent or its
own subtree, about symbols it already has. Every decision is **local** - so
long reach can only exist as a **chain** of one-hop decisions, each made by a
module the symbol had reached.

That chain is exactly what `invoicing` builds. `invoicingLibrary` exposes
`InvoiceModel` to its parent; `invoicing`, which now has the symbol
available, exposes it to its descendants - and the exposure reaches
`invoiceComputation` and `invoicePDF`, the owner's own siblings. Two
decisions, and `invoicingLibrary` works as a **domain library**: its type is
shared across the domain and invisible outside it.

Read `invoiceComputation`'s box: `InvoiceModel` arrives as `from invoicing` -
the row names the module that exposed it, not the owner. And read what is
_not_ there: `InvoiceModel` never arrives in `app`, because `invoicing`
exposed it to its descendants only. A domain keeps its vocabulary to itself
unless it also decides to expose it to its parent. Select `InvoiceModel` and the whole story
plays out inside the domain: one hop up, a turn, and every arrival blinks
under `invoicing`.

### Step 3: `globalLibrary` - sharing globally

One concept is shared by both domains: Money. That's why we create a
`globalLibrary` module containing a `moneyUtils` module, with a
`computeTotal` function which we want to make globally available. By now the
pattern is predictable - there is no "global" switch, only a longer chain:
`moneyUtils` exposes `computeTotal` to its parent, `globalLibrary` re-exposes
it to its parent, and `app` exposes it to its descendants, turning the chain
downward into every branch. Here is the complete
app: nine modules, four symbols, and seven dots that are its entire access
policy - complexity is measured in decisions, not boxes.

<div className="wide-figure">
  <ModelDiagram definition={example1Diagram} />
</div>

Now the punchline. Three leaf modules made the identical decision - expose
one symbol to their parent - and ended up with three different reaches.
`computeTotal` is re-exposed to the parent once more and then to descendants
at `app`, so every module may import it. `InvoiceModel` is exposed to
descendants one level lower, at `invoicing`, so its two consumers share it
and nothing outside that subtree can. `optimizeRoute` is composed by
`shipping` and exposed no further, so it goes one hop and stops. None of the
three owners decided that; the modules above them did. Exposing a symbol to
the parent cedes its onward exposure.

The full picture also shows what never happens. `shipping` may not import
`InvoiceModel`: an exposure to descendants never leaves the exposing module's
subtree, and `invoicing` did not expose the symbol to its parent, so nothing
outside its subtree ever sees it. The mirror case is the same rule read the
other way - `invoicing` may not import `optimizeRoute`, because `shipping`
exposed it nowhere. **There is no sibling channel.** Select either symbol and
the absence is the picture: the flow stops, and no arrival ever blinks in the
other domain.

A "global library" and a "domain library" are the same mechanism at different
altitudes. `app` exposes `computeTotal` to its descendants and `invoicing`
exposes `InvoiceModel` to its descendants; the difference between
application-wide and domain-wide reach is which module made the identical
decision. The root is an ordinary module
whose subtree happens to be everything - it owns no code at all here, and
still carries the application's vocabulary.

## Example 2: Both channels at once

In this smaller app, we keep the pricing rules in a `pricing` module, with
order submission in a `checkout` module beside it - and `PriceModel` is
wanted on both sides of `pricing`'s boundary: by its own sub-modules and by
the app around it.

One owner, one symbol, both exposure decisions. `pricing` owns `PriceModel`
and exposes it to its descendants _and_ to its parent - the first `▲▼`
marker in the series, and it is not a third channel: two independent one-hop
decisions that happen to share a row. The exposure to descendants reaches
`discounts` and `taxes`; the exposure to the parent reaches `app`, which
receives the type and stops it.

The lesson is the ceiling. Both channels at once buy exactly the owner's
subtree plus its parent - structurally the most any owner can reach alone.
`checkout`, the sibling that would most plausibly want `PriceModel`, is not
allowed to import it: crossing to a sibling was never `pricing`'s decision
to make, and `app` made none. Select `PriceModel` and both flows march out
of a single row - up one hop, down one subtree - and stop.

Reach ends where decisions end. There are three dots in this picture, all of
them on `pricing` and `checkout`; `app` has none, and both symbols stop
there. (`submitOrder` plays the role known from Example 1's `optimizeRoute` -
exposed to the parent, composed, stopped - and wears that symbol's color.)

## Two footnotes

**Exposure to descendants is uniform.** It reaches every branch of the
exposing module's subtree - including the branch the symbol originally came
up through: `app`'s exposure of `computeTotal` lands in `globalLibrary` and
`moneyUtils` too, the very branch that sent it up (the figure omits those two
arrival rows, because the exposure changes nothing there - both boxes already
had the symbol). No exclusion is possible, and re-exposed symbols carry no
provenance. A family that does not want its symbol coming back from an
ancestor has exactly one option: not exposing it to the parent at all.

**Subdivision is invisible.** Exposure to descendants addresses a subtree,
not a shape. Splitting `routingOptimization` into two sub-modules would
change nothing about what either half may import: `shipping`'s exposure of
`ShipmentPlan` addresses its subtree, whatever shape that subtree takes.
Structure can be refined freely without renegotiating anybody's access.

## Where the precision lives

The tree rules on this page are half the complete rule; the other half is on
the [tags](/tags) page, where two more example universes keep the tree
trivial and let a tag be the only variable.

The examples are teaching material. The normative document is the
cross-module importability rules specification, which ships with the
repository at `docs/model/cross-module-importability-rules.md`, with the
vocabulary defined in the [glossary](/glossary). Where a page and the
specification appear to disagree, the specification is right.
